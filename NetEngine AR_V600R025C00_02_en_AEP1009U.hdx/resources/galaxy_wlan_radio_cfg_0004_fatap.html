Channel

Basic Concepts

A data transmission channel on a WLAN uses radio signals as transmission media. For a WLAN, the coverage area of a single AP is limited. As such, multiple APs are often required to completely cover the target area. This deployment, however, may easily generate signal interference between APs. To avoid interference in this case, ensure that adjacent APs are configured to work on non-overlapping channels. Additionally, channel bonding technology can be used to improve the network speeds of STAs.

Radio signals of different frequency bands operate on different channels.

For channels supported in different countries, see WLAN Country Codes and Channels Compliance. You can also search for this document at Huawei technical support website.

Channels on the 2.4 GHz frequency band

In 802.11 standards, the 2.4 GHz frequency band can be divided into fourteen 20 MHz channels, with the channel ID ranging from 1 to 14 and adjacent channels overlapping, as shown in Figure 1. Currently, it is recommended that cellular-like WLAN coverage be deployed based on channels spaced by at least four channels, such as channels 1, 6, and 11.

Channels on the 5 GHz and 6 GHz frequency bands

Richer spectrum resources are available on the 5 GHz and 6 GHz frequency bands, enabling APs to work on 40 MHz, 80 MHz, or larger-bandwidth channels in addition to 20 MHz channels, as shown in the following figures.

In some regions, radar systems work on the 5 GHz frequency band, which may interfere with radio signals of APs working on the 5 GHz frequency band. Therefore, radar channels need to be avoided during channel planning.

Channel bonding

To improve the network speeds of STAs, you can increase the bandwidth for radio channels. For example, two 20 MHz channels can be bonded into a 40 MHz channel to send data to a STA. In this manner, theoretically, the bandwidth of the data transmission channel is doubled, meaning that the network speed is also doubled. If two 40 MHz channels are bonded, the network speed is also doubled. Based on different channel bonding methods, operating bandwidths can be classified as 40 MHz plus, 40 MHz minus, 80 MHz, 80+80 MHz, 160 MHz, and 320 MHz. As shown in Figure 2 and Figure 3, channels that can be bonded in pairs are fixed.
  • 40 MHz plus and 40 MHz minus: Two adjacent 20 MHz channels that do not interfere with each other are bonded into a 40 MHz channel. One 20 MHz channel is the primary channel, and the other is the auxiliary channel. The primary channel and auxiliary channel have different center frequencies, which determine the minus or plus state of the 40 MHz channel. If the center frequency of the former is higher, the channel bandwidth is 40 MHz minus; if that of the latter is higher, the channel bandwidth is 40 MHz plus. For example, 20 MHz channels 36 and 40 are bonded into a 40 MHz channel. If 40 MHz minus is configured, channel 40 needs to be deployed as the primary channel; if 40 MHz plus is configured, channel 36 needs to be deployed as the primary channel.

    In most cases, it is not recommended that the channel bandwidth of 40 MHz be configured on the 2.4 GHz frequency band because only one non-overlapping 40 MHz channel can be created on this frequency band. For example, after channels 1 and 5 are bonded into a 40 MHz channel (channel 1 overlaps with channels 2, 3, and 4), bonding the remaining channels must avoid channels 1 to 8 because channel 5 overlaps with channels 6, 7, and 8. As such, the remaining channels cannot be bonded into another 40 MHz channel.

  • 80 MHz: Two contiguous 40 MHz channels can be bonded into an 80 MHz channel. Any of the four 20 MHz channels in the 80 MHz channel can be selected as the primary channel. For example, channels 36, 40, 44, and 48 can be bonded into an 80 MHz channel.
  • 80+80 MHz: Two non-contiguous 80 MHz channels can be bonded into an 80+80 MHz channel. For example, channels 36, 40, 44, 48, 100, 104, 108, and 112 can be bonded into an 80+80 MHz channel.
  • 160 MHz: Two contiguous 80 MHz channels can be bonded into a 160 MHz channel. Any of the eight 20 MHz channels can be selected as the primary channel. For example, channels 36, 40, 44, 48, 52, 56, 60, and 64 can be bonded into a 160 MHz channel.
  • 320 MHz: Two neighboring 160 MHz channels can be bonded into a 320 MHz channel. In a 320 MHz channel, one 20 MHz channel must be configured as the primary channel. The other 20 MHz channels in the 160 MHz channel containing the primary channel are called the secondary 20 MHz channels. The 40 MHz channels that do not contain the primary channel are called the secondary 40 MHz channels. The 80 MHz channels that do not contain the primary channel are called the secondary 80 MHz channels. The 160 MHz channel that does not contain the primary channel is called the secondary 160 MHz channel. At most one 320 MHz channel (operating with fixed bandwidth of 240 MHz) is supported on the 5 GHz frequency band, and three 320 MHz channels are supported on the 6 GHz frequency band.

The primary channel is used to transmit management packets and control packets. The entire channel is idle only when the primary channel is idle.

Considering that the 6 GHz frequency band supports a large number of channels, it will consume a long time for a STA to scan each channel and set up a connection with an AP. As such, Wi-Fi 6E defines the preferred scanning channels (PSCs), including channels 5, 21, 37, 53, 69, 85, 101, 117, 133, 149, 165, 181, 197, 213, and 229. When an AP goes online for the first time or performs radio calibration, it preferentially selects a PSC channel, which improves the 6 GHz signal scanning efficiency.

Channel Adjustment

Channel adjustment ensures that each AP is allocated an optimal channel to minimize interference from adjacent channels or the same channel, ensuring reliable network transmission.

As shown in Figure 4, both AP2 and AP4 work on channel 6, leading to signal interference. After channel adjustment, AP4 is switched to channel 11, so that neighboring APs work on non-overlapping channels, eliminating interference.

Figure 4 Channel adjustment

In addition to radio calibration, channel adjustment can also be used for dynamic frequency selection (DFS). In some regions, radar systems work on the 5 GHz frequency band, which may interfere with radio signals of APs working on the 5 GHz frequency band. The DFS function enables APs to automatically switch to other channels when they detect interference on their current working channels.

During the DFS process, radar signals may be incorrectly determined. If radar signals are detected occasionally on a single AP, it may be a mistake. If radar signals are detected for multiple times on an AP or simultaneously on multiple APs, the radar signals may be determined. Then the APs can process the signals accordingly. To minimize false detection, all Huawei AP models are optimized for DFS false detection to distinguish radar signals and non-radar signals more accurately.
