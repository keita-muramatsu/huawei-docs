Understanding FEC

FEC Process

Figure 1 shows the FEC process.

Figure 1 FEC process
  1. When receiving packets, the transmit end discovers that original packets 1 to 4 require FEC processing.
  2. The transmit end performs FEC encoding on original packets.
    1. The transmit end encapsulates original packets 1 to 4 with a proprietary FEC header.
    2. The transmit end treats original packets 1 to 4 as an encoding block, and performs FEC encoding on the encoding block to generate redundant packets R1, R2, and R3 based on the generator matrix G.
  3. Original packets and redundant packets are transmitted on the network. During this process, some original packets and redundant packets may be lost. The lost packets in an encoding block can be recovered as long as the number of lost packets in the encoding block is less than or equal to the number of redundant packets received by the receive end.
  4. The receive end performs FEC decoding on the received original packets and redundant packets.
    1. When receiving packets, the receive end detects that the received packets include original packets 2 and 4, as well as redundant packets R2 and R3. The receive end then recovers the lost original packets 1 and 3 based on recovery matrix R and received packets.
    2. The receive end removes the proprietary header to obtain original packets 1 to 4.
  5. The receive end sends original packets 1 to 4 in sequence to other devices.

FEC Classification

FEC is classified as either Determined FEC (D-FEC) or Adaptive FEC (A-FEC) based on the algorithm used for generating redundant packets.

D-FEC generates redundant packets based on a fixed redundancy rate, where:

Redundancy rate = Number of redundant packets/Number of original packets

As shown in Figure 2, a fixed redundancy rate may cause the following problems:

  • If the packet loss rate is less than the redundancy rate, bandwidth resources are wasted.

    When the packet loss rate is low, more redundant packets than required are generated, wasting network bandwidth resources or even affecting other services in the case of network congestion.

  • If the packet loss rate is greater than the redundancy rate, lost packets cannot be recovered.

    Packet loss on the network may occur continuously for a short period of time. If the transient packet loss rate is greater than the redundancy rate of D-FEC, redundant packets generated in a short period of time will not be sufficient for recovering lost packets.

Figure 2 Disadvantages of D-FEC

A-FEC, an improvement of D-FEC, dynamically generates redundant packets based on the packet loss information returned by the decoding end. It resolves the problems of D-FEC, such as bandwidth waste and failures to recover lost packets. As shown in Figure 3, A-FEC dynamically adjusts the FEC redundancy rate based on the packet loss rate. If the packet loss rate is low, A-FEC decreases the redundancy rate to reduce bandwidth consumption. Conversely, if the packet loss rate is high, A-FEC increases the redundancy rate to correct for the packet loss. However, A-FEC takes time to detect packet loss changes and dynamically adjust the packet redundancy rate. As such, if the packet loss rate suddenly changes drastically, A-FEC cannot respond in time, meaning that some lost packets cannot be recovered.

Figure 3 A-FEC process

To improve application experience, you are advised to select the FEC mode based on the network bandwidth. If the network bandwidth is sufficient, the network quality is poor, and the packet loss rate keeps changing, use D-FEC. If the network bandwidth is low, the network quality is poor, and the packet loss rate does not fluctuate significantly, use A-FEC. Table 1 compares A-FEC and D-FEC.
